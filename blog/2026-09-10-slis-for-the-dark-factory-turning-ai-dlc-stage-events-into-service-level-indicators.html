    SLIs for the Dark Factory: Turning AI-DLC Stage Events into Service Level Indicators

    A lights-out software factory still needs SLIs. Learn how to turn AI-DLC gate, sensor and bolt events into good-over-valid ratios your team can measure and defend.

    Lights Out on the Factory Floor

    Manufacturing has a name for a plant that runs without people in the building: the dark factory. The lights stay off because nothing on the floor needs to see. Humans set the tolerances, watch the instrumentation from somewhere else, and walk in only when something needs a decision a machine should not make.

    That is the shape a lot of software organisations are now building toward. Agents pick up an intent, elaborate requirements, decompose the work, write the code, run the tests and open the pull request. The humans are still accountable, but they are not in the loop for every step — they are watching the instruments.

    Which raises the obvious problem. A dark factory with no instrumentation is just a dark room. Before you can safely take your hands off the controls, you need to know what "working" means in numbers, and that means SLIs.

    Define the Journey First

    The discipline here is exactly the one we teach for user-facing services: start from the critical user journey, not from the metrics you happen to have. The journey for a delivery factory is short to state and hard to measure:

    A developer describes an intent. Some time later, a reviewed, tested change is merged, and the developer did not have to rescue it along the way.

    Everything worth measuring hangs off that sentence. "Some time later" is your latency SLI. "Reviewed and tested" is your quality SLI. "Did not have to rescue it" is your autonomy SLI, and it is the one most teams skip because it is uncomfortable.

    The Method Already Asks for This

    Before you start inventing metrics, it is worth knowing that the method already requires them. The AI-DLC method definition paper puts a ritual called Mob Elaboration at the heart of its Inception phase: the product owner, developers, QA and other stakeholders in one room with a shared screen, with AI proposing the initial breakdown of an Intent into user stories and Units while the humans correct the parts that are over- or under-engineered. The paper lists what that ritual must produce — a PRFAQ, user stories, non-functional requirement definitions, a description of risks matching the organisation's risk register, the suggested Bolts, and "Measurement Criteria that traces to the business intent".

    That last item is an SLI in all but name, and it is demanded at exactly the right moment: while the humans are still in the room agreeing what the work is for, and before anyone has written code that would be awkward to instrument. The failure mode is not that the method forgets to ask. It is that "measurement criteria" gets written down as a sentence — "the recommendation engine should be fast and reliable" — when what was needed was a ratio with a denominator and a query that returns it.

    The Events Are Already There

    AI-DLC makes this unusually tractable because its engine writes a typed audit trail as it runs — 99 event types across workflow, phase, stage, gate, sensor, unit, bolt and swarm lifecycles. You are not instrumenting from scratch; you are choosing which of the existing rows to count.

    Good SLIs are ratios of good events over valid events, as the SRE Workbook puts it. Here are five that hold up in practice.

    1. Gate First-Pass Rate

    Of the approval gates presented, how many were approved without the stage being sent back?

    gate_first_pass =
    count(GATE_APPROVED where revisions == 0)
  / count(GATE_APPROVED + GATE_REJECTED)

    This is the closest thing to a quality SLI you will get from the harness itself, because the judge is a human who had to live with the result. A falling first-pass rate is the earliest warning that a prompt change, a model change or a new codebase has degraded output quality.

    2. Rework Ratio

    AI-DLC emits STAGE_REVISING when a stage goes back around. Count revisions against stage completions:

    rework_ratio = count(STAGE_REVISING) / count(STAGE_COMPLETED)

    Track it per stage, not just in aggregate. A factory-wide rework ratio of 0.2 sounds tolerable until you discover it is 0.9 concentrated entirely in Domain Design, at which point you have a specific, fixable problem instead of a vague feeling that "the AI is not great at architecture".

    3. Sensor Pass Rate

    Sensors are AI-DLC's deterministic verification checks — linting, type checking and similar — that fire on matching edits or at the approval gate and record SENSOR_FIRED, SENSOR_PASSED and SENSOR_FAILED rows. The ratio of passes to fires is a correctness SLI with no model in the judging seat, which makes it the most trustworthy number on this list.

    4. Unit Convergence Rate

    When work is decomposed into units and dispatched in parallel, some units converge and some fail. SWARM_UNIT_CONVERGED over SWARM_UNIT_CONVERGED + SWARM_UNIT_FAILED tells you whether your parallelism is real throughput or just expensive churn. If the convergence rate drops below about 0.9, the decomposition is wrong more often than the code is.

    5. Intent Lead Time

    The latency SLI, and the one that maps most directly onto the method's own vocabulary. AI-DLC decomposes an Intent into Units — loosely coupled, independently deployable work elements, analogous to subdomains in DDD or epics in Scrum — and each Unit is built across one or more Bolts. The paper rebrands the sprint as the Bolt precisely to signal the change in cadence: where a sprint ran four to six weeks, a Bolt is the smallest iteration, measured in hours or days.

    So measure at both levels. Bolt duration tells you whether the cadence the method promises is real in your organisation. Intent lead time — elapsed time from WORKFLOW_STARTED to WORKFLOW_COMPLETED, as a distribution reported at p50 and p90 — tells you what a stakeholder actually experiences. Split it into machine time and human-wait time at gates. Most teams discover that the harness is fast and the queue in front of a reviewer is not, which is a useful thing to discover before you spend a quarter optimising prompts.

    Set Targets You Can Defend

    Do not invent targets. Run the factory for a few weeks with the SLIs collected and nothing else, then set the objective just above observed performance so that the first month's error budget is tight but not already spent. The error budget calculator makes the arithmetic concrete: a 95% gate first-pass SLO over 200 gates a month gives you ten rejections to spend before the objective is breached — a number a team can actually reason about, unlike "the AI should mostly get it right".

    Write the definitions down where they can be reviewed. The same argument that applies to SLOs as code for production services applies here, and more forcefully: a factory SLO that lives in one person's dashboard will drift the moment that person changes teams.

    What This Buys You

    An instrumented factory turns arguments into measurements. "The agents are getting worse" becomes a first-pass rate with a date on it. "We should add another approval gate" becomes a question about which stage actually carries the rework. And most importantly, it gives you the raw material for the next step: deciding, from evidence rather than nerve, how much autonomy the factory has earned.
